# Evidence-bounded autonomy in agentic software development

> Prepared with Codex assistance and reviewed by the student for final submission.

Good agentic development should increase autonomy only as the evidence supporting a change becomes stronger. The relevant achievement is not how long an agent runs without interruption, but whether another person can inspect why its output should be trusted. This shifts evaluation from producing code to producing a defensible change: implementation, appropriate checks, and a bounded account of what remains unknown. It also leaves human judgement responsible for deciding which outcomes matter.

Productivity evidence makes unconditional claims about autonomy difficult. GitHub’s [2022 controlled experiment](https://github.blog/news-insights/research/research-quantifying-github-copilots-impact-on-developer-productivity-and-happiness/) reported substantially faster completion with Copilot on a JavaScript HTTP-server task. That supports assistance for a bounded construction exercise, but not automatically for unfamiliar production systems or long-term maintenance. As vendor research, it also warrants attention to the selected task and success measure rather than accepting the headline as a universal property of AI programming.

By contrast, [Becker and colleagues at METR (2025)](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) found experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools on tasks in their own repositories. This is not simply a refutation of GitHub: the tools, participants and tasks differ. Established repositories impose conventions and contextual knowledge that a short exercise may not capture. Crucially, METR’s [February 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) warns that later estimates are compromised by participation and task-selection effects. It would therefore be equally misleading to use the earlier slowdown as proof that current agents cannot help. Together, these studies motivate measuring outcomes in their actual setting rather than inferring value from activity or confidence.

Long-running agents add a continuity problem. [Liu et al. (2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/) found that relevant information’s position affects performance in long-context retrieval and question-answering tasks. This is not a direct experiment on software repositories, and should not be presented as one. Nevertheless, it challenges the assumption that accumulating instructions guarantees their effective use. My inference is that a development harness needs concise current constraints and retrievable historical evidence, not an indefinitely expanding document where active rules and obsolete reports become indistinguishable.

[Anthropic’s practitioner account (2025)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) proposes incremental work, durable progress artifacts and testing to bridge context windows. This is valuable operational guidance from building agents, but not an independent demonstration that any particular harness guarantees reliable software. A progress file can preserve an unsupported conclusion just as efficiently as a verified result. Continuity must therefore preserve evidence boundaries: which version ran, what environment was used, what was observed, and what was inferred. Otherwise a plausible summary becomes the next agent’s mistaken premise.

A defensible workflow gives different claims different checks. A transaction invariant needs executable tests; an interaction needs a browser; a claim about two people hearing each other needs an actual audio path and ultimately human confirmation. An agent that authored both implementation and tests may reproduce the same misunderstanding in both. Independent review helps, but a second agent reading only the first agent’s summary is not independent evidence. It must inspect the artifact or observe a relevant consequence. Human review should similarly target questions that automated checks cannot settle, rather than ceremonially approve a green badge.

The strongest counterargument is cost. Requiring exhaustive evidence for every edit could erase the productivity gains the tools offer. Evidence-bounded autonomy is therefore proportional: a text correction deserves a small check, while account recovery, cross-user permissions and destructive actions deserve stronger scrutiny. Repeated failures should improve a reusable invariant or narrow scope, not generate endless reruns. This preserves useful delegation without confusing a long autonomous session with a successful one.

These sources do not establish one universally optimal harness. They support a more modest position: autonomy is an engineering allocation whose benefits depend on task, context and verification cost. Teams should report those conditions and their uncertainty. The future worth pursuing is not software development without judgement, but development where increasingly capable agents make the grounds for human judgement easier to inspect.
